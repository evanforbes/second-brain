## Summary

Google rewards **helpful, reliable, people-first content** and demotes content made mainly to rank. Its Search Quality Rater Guidelines (182 pages; they train human raters, not the algorithm directly) describe what "high quality" means. The core concept is **E-E-A-T: Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trust. Trust is the most important member,** because untrustworthy pages have low E-E-A-T no matter how expert they seem.

## YMYL: the standard that applies to Underdog

- **"Your Money or Your Life" (YMYL) topics** could significantly affect people's health, **financial stability**, safety, or society's welfare.
- YMYL pages are held to the **highest** standard of trust and reputation.
- Real-money products and prediction and trading content sit squarely in the financial-stability zone. The rater guidelines also use **gambling, payday loans, and lottery content** as recurring examples of low-trust and abuse patterns.

What raters look for, and so what to show clearly:
- **Who is responsible** for the site and content: About us, legal entity, contact and customer service.
- **The creators' reputation and experience:** author bylines and credentials, and what others say about the site and its creators (reviews, news, independent sources).
- **The main content's quality:** effort, originality, accuracy, and whether it satisfies the page's purpose.
- **For financial topics,** accuracy and transparency: terms, risks, fees, and eligibility.

## Helpful content: Google's self-assessment

- Is it written **for people first,** from real knowledge or experience, with a clear purpose?
- Does it provide original information, analysis, or insight **beyond the obvious**?
- Would a reader leave satisfied, or need to search again?
- **Who, how, why:** make authorship clear; explain how content was produced (including any automation) where readers would expect it; and the "why" should be to help people, not to manipulate rankings.

**On AI-generated content:** Google says using AI isn't against its guidelines. **Using it to mass-produce pages without adding value is** (see scaled content abuse below).

## Spam policies with the most risk for this vertical

| Policy | What it is | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| **Scaled content abuse** | Many pages generated mainly to manipulate rankings, however they're made (AI, scraping, stitching, templating) | Auto-generating a page for every player, game, or state with no unique value |
| **Site reputation abuse** | Third-party content placed on a trusted host to borrow its ranking signals | A classic gambling and affiliate tactic (sponsored "best apps" pages on news sites) |
| **Expired domain abuse** | Buying an old domain to host unrelated content | Google's own example is **casino content on a former school site** |
| **Thin affiliation** | Affiliate pages copied from the merchant with no added value | Affiliate partners promoting Underdog |
| **Doorway abuse** | Many near-duplicate pages funneling users to one place | Near-identical city or state landing pages |
| **Link spam** | Buying or exchanging links to manipulate rank | Paid placements without `rel="sponsored"` |

**Penalties** range from algorithmic demotion to manual actions (visible in Search Console).

## Content that tends to earn visibility (SEO and AEO)

- **Clear, direct answers early in the page**, then depth. Descriptive headings, short paragraphs, lists and tables where they help. AI systems quote well-structured text (Microsoft's AI-search guidance says the same).
- **Original data and expert explanation** that others cite. That builds the off-site mentions AEO rewards.
- **Freshness** on time-sensitive topics (schedules, rules, regulations).
- **Entity clarity:** consistent brand, product, and organization naming everywhere, including profiles and directories.
